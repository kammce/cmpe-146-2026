How it works

No shared clock. SPI and I²C both carry a clock line, so the receiver just reads bits exactly when the controller says to. UART carries no clock at all — each side is only wired to send data (TX) into the other's RX pin. Both sides have to already agree, in advance, on a baud rate (bits per second), because that agreement is the only way the receiver knows how long one bit lasts.

The frame. A UART line idles high. To send a byte, the transmitter pulls the line low for one bit period (the start bit) — that falling edge is the only synchronization the receiver ever gets. Then come the 8 data bits, least-significant bit first, each held for one bit period at the transmitter's own baud rate. Finally a stop bit (line back to idle-high) closes the frame.

How the receiver samples. The instant it sees the falling edge, the receiver starts its own internal timer, ticking at its own configured baud rate — not the transmitter's. It waits half a bit period to land in the middle of the start bit, then samples again every full bit period after that: middle of data bit 0, middle of data bit 1, and so on, finishing with the stop bit. It never re-checks itself against the wire again until the next start bit. If both sides agree on the baud rate, every sample lands dead center in the right bit. If they don't, the receiver's sample points drift a little further from center on every single bit, because the error compounds — by the 9th or 10th bit, a small per-bit error can add up to enough drift to land the sample in the wrong bit entirely, silently corrupting that bit, or missing the stop bit altogether (a framing error, the classic symptom of a baud-rate mismatch).

Try it: leave both baud rates matched and send a byte — perfect reception every time. Nudge MCU B's rate by 1% and send again; still usually fine, since the drift by the last bit is still a small fraction of a bit period. Push it to 5% or 20% and watch the sample dots on the trace visibly drift away from the center of each bit, and the received byte (and the per-bit comparison below it) start disagreeing with what was actually sent — sometimes with a framing error, sometimes just silently wrong data, depending on exactly which bits the drift happens to land on.
